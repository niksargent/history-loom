# Does history rhyme?

The original British collection explicitly divides 1066–2025 into twelve fixed 80-year periods: see `docs/classic/research-notes.md`. It is therefore unsuitable as independent evidence that crises recur every 80 years. Its pressure scores are editorial interpretations, not measurements from an independently coded historical panel.

Strauss and Howe's Fourth Turning proposes a roughly 80–100-year generational rhythm, principally through an Anglo-American lens. The atlas treats this as a hypothesis to question. The original event-selection demo has been replaced by **Echoes across time**, which gives each comparison a basis in the six editorial pressure scores. Four deterministic k-means groups organise all 32 original eras; dates and country are excluded from the grouping features. Four groups are a display choice, not a claim that history has four natural classes. Ring positions are for readability and do not encode distance.

Select any era to see its three nearest comparisons, ranked by the same mean absolute score distance as the unchanged fingerprint view. The country control filters comparisons only. The timeline shows full era durations and gaps between start years. An optional 80-year ruler is anchored to the chosen era's start. These irregular gaps show the dates of similar editorial profiles in this collection; they do not constitute an independent test of a cycle, especially given Britain's fixed bins and unequal era lengths elsewhere. There is no significance threshold, prediction, or probability presented.

A defensible recurrence study would define outcomes before inspecting the dates; code events independently across societies; handle date uncertainty and missingness; compare candidate rhythms against a null model; account for multiple searches; and test on held-out periods. The app does not pretend that its small, curated event set meets those requirements.

Three different kinds of evidence remain visibly distinct:

1. **Historical comparisons:** sourced narratives connected by editorial tags. Useful for questions about networks, power, copying, public participation, and recovery; not quantitative evidence of causation.
2. **Editorial fingerprints:** six original v1 dimensions, matched using mean absolute score distance. Similar scores may invite a comparison, but inherit the original scoring judgments and are not probabilities or predictions.
3. **Observed estimates:** the real life-expectancy series from [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/life-expectancy). Its long-run changes and uneven setbacks offer an important counterpoint to a repeating-history story. Period life expectancy describes mortality rates in a given year; it is not a forecast of the lifespan of a particular child. Historical estimates and measurement coverage vary.

The [Seshat Global History Databank](https://seshatdatabank.info/data) is a promising future source for independently structured comparative historical research. It was researched, but is not imported or represented as data in this release. Incorporating it responsibly needs a versioned published dataset, variables and geographic units suited to a specified question, and treatment of uncertainty and alternate codings.

Every moment exposes its own institutional sources and a specific comparison limit. Ancient dates are flagged as approximate. Present-day coastlines orient the viewer and do not depict historical borders. Map curves mean shared themes, not routes taken. The visual world is imagined; it is not photographic evidence.
